# Literature Review: Economic Valuation of Wildfire Smoke-Attributable ED Visits in California

**Date:** 2026-04-04
**Query:** What is the economic impact of wildfire smoke-attributable ED visits in California, and are BenMAP-CE's cost-of-illness (COI) unit values the most appropriate valuation method?

## Summary

Wildfire smoke has become one of the most pressing environmental health threats in the western United States, and California in particular faces a growing burden of smoke-attributable emergency department visits. The epidemiological evidence linking wildfire PM2.5 to respiratory, cardiovascular, and other acute health outcomes is now firmly established, with studies consistently documenting elevated ED visit rates during smoke events (Chen et al. 2023; Wettstein et al. 2018; Heft-Neal et al. 2023). The central policy question is no longer whether wildfire smoke harms health, but how to accurately quantify the resulting economic costs so that benefit-cost analyses of wildfire management investments and air quality regulations reflect the true burden.

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency's BenMAP-CE tool is the standard instrument for monetizing air pollution health impacts in regulatory analyses, and several studies have applied it to wildfire contexts (Fann et al. 2018; Jones 2016; Jones and Berrens 2017). However, BenMAP-CE's default unit values for morbidity endpoints such as ED visits rely predominantly on cost-of-illness (COI) measures -- direct medical expenditures and lost earnings -- which economic theory has long recognized as a lower bound on true willingness to pay (WTP) for health risk reductions (Harrington and Portney 1987). A growing body of empirical evidence quantifies several specific dimensions of this underestimation: Richardson et al. (2012, 2013) find that WTP for avoiding wildfire smoke morbidity exceeds COI by approximately 9:1; Birnbaum et al. (2020) show that BenMAP-CE misses roughly 40% of healthcare costs by excluding ambulatory care; Meng et al. (2024) find that BenMAP-CE underestimates wildfire-specific work-loss costs in California by 5--10 times; and Han et al. (2024) document significant defensive spending that COI measures ignore entirely.

Despite this accumulating evidence, no existing study has synthesized all dimensions of BenMAP-CE underestimation for a single health endpoint in a single jurisdiction. This review organizes the literature across five thematic areas -- the BenMAP-CE and EPA valuation framework, the COI versus WTP methodological debate, wildfire smoke epidemiology, broader economic valuation of air pollution health effects, and environmental justice -- to establish the foundation for a comprehensive assessment of whether BenMAP-CE's unit values are "the reasonable and best fit" for valuing California's wildfire smoke-attributable ED visits.

## Key Papers

### Chen et al. (2023) -- Emergency Department Visits Associated with Wildfire Smoke Events in California, 2016--2019
- **Main contribution:** Provides the foundational epidemiological estimates of wildfire smoke-attributable ED visits that this capstone monetizes.
- **Method:** Time-stratified case-crossover design using OSHPD ED visit data linked to air basin-level wildfire smoke events (defined as days with wildfire PM2.5 at or above the 98th percentile, 13.5 ug/m3). Examines multiple lag structures.
- **Key finding:** Smoke events associated with 14.4% increased risk of respiratory ED visits (lag 1), 57.1% increase for asthma (lag 0), and 12.7% for chronic lower respiratory disease (lag 0). Conducted by OEHHA/CalEPA researchers.
- **Relevance:** This is the anchor study. The capstone applies economic unit values to the excess ED visits estimated by Chen et al. to produce dollar-denominated cost figures.

### Richardson et al. (2012) -- The Hidden Cost of Wildfires: Economic Valuation of Health Effects of Wildfire Smoke Exposure in Southern California
- **Main contribution:** Provides the first direct comparison of COI and WTP for wildfire smoke morbidity, demonstrating a dramatic gap between the two.
- **Method:** Defensive behavior (revealed preference) method applied to individual-level expenditure data during a major Los Angeles County wildfire. COI calculated from medical costs and lost earnings.
- **Key finding:** WTP of $84.42 per person per day versus COI of $9.50 per person per day -- a ratio of nearly 9:1.
- **Relevance:** This is the single most important methodological reference for the central argument that BenMAP-CE's reliance on COI substantially underestimates true welfare costs. The 9:1 ratio provides the primary WTP adjustment factor.

### Richardson et al. (2013) -- Valuing Morbidity from Wildfire Smoke Exposure: A Comparison of Revealed and Stated Preference Techniques
- **Main contribution:** Provides convergent validity for the WTP estimates by comparing two independent valuation methods applied to the same wildfire event.
- **Method:** Both defensive behavior (revealed preference, $87/symptom day) and contingent valuation (stated preference, $95/symptom day) applied to the 2009 Station Fire. COI estimates of $3 (traditional) and $17 (comprehensive) serve as comparators.
- **Key finding:** WTP estimates from two independent methods are not statistically different from each other but are dramatically higher than COI. This convergence strengthens the case that the WTP-COI gap is real, not an artifact of a single method.
- **Relevance:** Together with Richardson et al. (2012), this establishes the empirical basis for arguing that BenMAP-CE's COI-only approach misses the majority of welfare costs from wildfire smoke morbidity.

### Birnbaum et al. (2020) -- Measuring the Impact of Air Pollution on Health Care Costs
- **Main contribution:** Identifies a specific structural gap in BenMAP-CE's cost accounting: the exclusion of ambulatory care costs.
- **Method:** Analysis of real-world insurance claims data to compare actual healthcare costs associated with air pollution-related illness to the cost categories captured in BenMAP-CE.
- **Key finding:** BenMAP-CE underestimates healthcare costs by approximately 40% because it counts only hospital admissions and ED visits while excluding physician visits, prescriptions, and home health. Every hospitalized respiratory patient averaged five additional ambulatory visits not captured.
- **Relevance:** Even within the COI framework, BenMAP-CE misses substantial costs. This is an underestimation dimension independent of the WTP-COI gap.

### Meng et al. (2024) -- Health and Economic Cost Estimates of Short-Term Total and Wildfire PM2.5 Exposure on Work Loss
- **Main contribution:** Demonstrates that BenMAP-CE dramatically underestimates wildfire-specific work-loss costs in California.
- **Method:** Links California Health Interview Survey (CHIS) respondents to daily PM2.5 and wildfire-specific PM2.5, estimating work-loss days and associated economic losses.
- **Key finding:** Wildfire smoke contributes 0.7--2.6 million work-loss days annually in California, with economic losses of $129--521 million. BenMAP-CE underestimates wildfire work-loss by 5--10x.
- **Relevance:** California-specific evidence that BenMAP-CE's underestimation extends beyond medical costs to productivity losses. While focused on work-loss rather than ED visits, the finding supports the broader argument that BenMAP-CE systematically undervalues wildfire smoke morbidity.

### Harrington and Portney (1987) -- Valuing the Benefits of Health and Safety Regulation
- **Main contribution:** Provides the foundational theoretical argument that COI is a lower bound on WTP for health risk reductions.
- **Method:** Theoretical analysis of the welfare components captured and omitted by the COI approach.
- **Key finding:** COI captures only direct medical costs and lost earnings, systematically omitting pain and suffering, defensive expenditures, risk aversion, and other welfare components. Produced under contract with EPA's Office of Air Quality Planning and Standards.
- **Relevance:** This is the theoretical backbone. It explains why the empirical findings of Richardson et al. (2012, 2013) -- the large WTP-COI gap -- are exactly what theory predicts, not anomalies.

### EPA (2023) -- BenMAP-CE User's Manual: Appendices
- **Main contribution:** Primary technical documentation for BenMAP-CE, including Appendix H on health valuation functions.
- **Method:** Documents the default unit cost values for ED visits, hospital admissions, and other endpoints, their derivation, and underlying assumptions.
- **Key finding:** Specifies the COI-based default values that constitute the baseline valuation in this capstone.
- **Relevance:** The object of analysis. Understanding what BenMAP-CE does and does not capture is the prerequisite for evaluating whether its values are appropriate.

### EPA (2024) -- Guidelines for Preparing Economic Analyses, 3rd Edition
- **Main contribution:** Establishes the official EPA methodological framework for health endpoint valuation in regulatory benefit-cost analysis.
- **Method:** Comprehensive guideline document covering COI, averting behavior, and stated preference approaches.
- **Key finding:** Acknowledges that relatively few WTP estimates exist for many morbidity risks, making COI the default despite its known tendency to underestimate true welfare losses. Explicitly states the preference hierarchy: WTP-based measures are preferred when available.
- **Relevance:** EPA's own guidelines acknowledge the limitations of the COI approach that BenMAP-CE employs, providing institutional support for the capstone's critique.

### Aguilera et al. (2021) -- Wildfire Smoke Impacts Respiratory Health More Than Fine Particles from Other Sources
- **Main contribution:** Establishes that wildfire-origin PM2.5 is more harmful per unit concentration than PM2.5 from other sources.
- **Method:** Observational analysis of respiratory hospitalizations in Southern California, comparing health impacts of wildfire versus non-wildfire PM2.5.
- **Key finding:** Wildfire PM2.5 causes 1.3--10% increases in respiratory hospitalizations per 10 ug/m3, versus only 0.67--1.3% for non-wildfire PM2.5. The differential toxicity factor is approximately 1.5--8x.
- **Relevance:** BenMAP-CE's default concentration-response functions do not distinguish between wildfire and non-wildfire PM2.5, meaning the tool likely underestimates health impacts -- and thus economic costs -- from wildfire smoke specifically.

### Heft-Neal et al. (2023) -- Emergency Department Visits Respond Nonlinearly to Wildfire Smoke
- **Main contribution:** Reveals a concave, nonlinear dose-response relationship between wildfire smoke and ED visits.
- **Method:** Analysis of U.S. ED visit data linked to daily smoke PM2.5 estimates at the zip-code level.
- **Key finding:** Total ED visits decline at extreme smoke concentrations due to behavioral avoidance, while respiratory-specific visits increase 30--110%. Linear dose-response models mischaracterize the health impact at high concentrations.
- **Relevance:** Two-fold implication. First, BenMAP-CE's linear concentration-response functions may misestimate health impacts. Second, behavioral avoidance itself is a welfare cost not captured by either COI or standard WTP measures.

### Kochi et al. (2016) -- Valuing Morbidity Effects of Wildfire Smoke Exposure from the 2007 Southern California Wildfires
- **Main contribution:** Provides a direct COI estimate of wildfire smoke ED visit costs in Southern California.
- **Method:** Analysis of 187 hospital facilities and 140 EDs in five Southern California counties during the 2007 wildfires.
- **Key finding:** Approximately 80 excess respiratory hospital admissions, 760 excess respiratory ED visits, 26 cardiovascular admissions, and 38 cardiovascular ED visits, with total medical costs exceeding $3.4 million.
- **Relevance:** The most direct COI-only precedent for the capstone's baseline analysis. The modest $3.4 million figure illustrates how COI alone produces cost estimates that appear small relative to the scale of health burden.

### Fann et al. (2018) -- The Health Impacts and Economic Value of Wildland Fire Episodes in the U.S.: 2008--2012
- **Main contribution:** Provides the most comprehensive national-scale BenMAP-CE application to wildfire smoke valuation.
- **Method:** CMAQ-modeled fire PM2.5, CDC WONDER baseline rates, and BenMAP-CE for economic valuation across cardiovascular, respiratory, and other outcomes.
- **Key finding:** Substantial national health impacts and costs from wildland fire episodes over the 2008--2012 period.
- **Relevance:** The direct methodological precedent for the capstone's BenMAP-CE application, though the capstone differs by treating BenMAP-CE's default values as the object of critique rather than accepting them at face value.

### Connolly et al. (2024) -- Mortality Attributable to PM2.5 from Wildland Fires in California from 2008 to 2018
- **Main contribution:** Provides a California-specific mortality valuation that contextualizes the scale of morbidity costs.
- **Method:** Applies wildfire-specific chronic dose-response coefficients to estimate premature deaths, valued using standard VSL.
- **Key finding:** 52,480--55,710 premature deaths attributable to wildland fire PM2.5 over 11 years, valued at $432--456 billion.
- **Relevance:** Establishes that mortality dominates the total economic burden of wildfire smoke in California. The capstone's morbidity estimates are a complement to, not a substitute for, this mortality valuation.

### Han et al. (2024) -- A Burning Issue: Wildfire Smoke Exposure, Retail Sales, and Demand for Adaptation in Healthcare
- **Main contribution:** Documents defensive spending as a measurable but uncounted component of wildfire smoke costs.
- **Method:** Analysis of NielsenIQ retail scanner data (2006--2019) linked to wildfire smoke exposure.
- **Key finding:** Wildfire smoke significantly increases retail sales of air purifiers, cold remedies, nasal products, and other health-related products. Effects vary by social vulnerability.
- **Relevance:** Defensive spending is captured by WTP but not by COI. This study provides direct evidence of a welfare cost category that BenMAP-CE ignores and demonstrates that adaptive capacity varies with socioeconomic status, connecting valuation methodology to environmental justice.

### Deryugina et al. (2019) -- The Mortality and Medical Costs of Air Pollution: Evidence from Changes in Wind Direction
- **Main contribution:** Provides gold-standard causal identification of air pollution health costs using an instrumental variables approach.
- **Method:** Wind direction as an instrument for PM2.5 exposure among elderly Medicare beneficiaries.
- **Key finding:** Causal effects of acute PM2.5 exposure on mortality, healthcare use, and medical costs, with mortality concentrated in about 25% of the elderly population.
- **Relevance:** Establishes the general framework for credibly estimating pollution-related health costs and demonstrates the magnitude of costs that emerge when causal identification is rigorous.

## Thematic Organization

### Theme 1: The BenMAP-CE Valuation Framework and Its Institutional Context

The EPA's BenMAP-CE tool occupies a central position in U.S. environmental regulatory analysis. It combines air quality modeling, concentration-response functions from the epidemiological literature, and economic unit values to produce monetized health benefit estimates for proposed regulations. The tool's user manual and appendices (EPA 2023) document its default unit cost values for health endpoints including ED visits, which are primarily derived from COI studies that tabulate direct medical expenditures and lost productivity.

EPA's own Guidelines for Preparing Economic Analyses (EPA 2024) explicitly acknowledge a valuation hierarchy in which WTP-based measures are preferred over COI when available, reflecting the long-standing theoretical understanding that COI represents a lower bound on welfare losses (Harrington and Portney 1987). The guidelines note, however, that relatively few WTP estimates exist for many morbidity endpoints, creating a practical reliance on COI in the absence of preferred alternatives. This institutional tension -- between the acknowledged superiority of WTP and the practical dominance of COI in BenMAP-CE -- is the foundational context for this capstone.

Several studies have applied BenMAP-CE directly to wildfire smoke contexts. Fann et al. (2018) produced the most comprehensive national assessment, estimating health impacts and economic values of wildland fire episodes from 2008 to 2012 using CMAQ-modeled fire PM2.5 and BenMAP-CE's default valuation functions. Jones (2016) applied BenMAP-CE to estimate health costs from the 2011 Wallow mega-fire in Arizona/New Mexico, finding a per capita WTP of $130 (2014 dollars) and demonstrating that substituting wildfire-specific concentration-response functions into BenMAP-CE substantially increases cost estimates relative to the defaults. Jones and Berrens (2017) extended this approach across the Western U.S. using a benefits transfer protocol, estimating average annual morbidity and mortality costs of $165 million with 217% annual growth between 2010 and 2015.

Dittrich and McCallum (2020) provide a systematic review of methods for measuring the economic health costs of wildfires in North America. Their review covers studies from 2006 onward, cataloging key methodological choices including the selection of cost estimators for mortality and morbidity effects. They conclude that all studies find highly significant health costs in the millions or billions of dollars, with mortality costs typically dominating the total. Their review provides important context for understanding the state of the field and the range of methodological approaches that have been applied.

### Theme 2: The COI-WTP Divergence -- Theory and Evidence

The theoretical case that COI understates the true welfare cost of illness was articulated by Harrington and Portney (1987), who demonstrated that COI captures only direct medical expenditures and foregone earnings while systematically omitting pain and suffering, defensive expenditures, risk aversion, and the disutility of illness itself. This theoretical prediction has been confirmed empirically in the wildfire smoke context with remarkable consistency.

Richardson et al. (2012) provide the landmark empirical estimate. Studying a major wildfire in Los Angeles County, they measure both COI ($9.50 per person per day in direct medical costs and lost wages) and WTP via the defensive behavior method ($84.42 per person per day), yielding a ratio of approximately 9:1. The defensive behavior approach captures actual market expenditures people make to avoid or mitigate smoke exposure -- air filters, medications, alternative shelter, lost recreation -- that COI measures ignore.

Richardson et al. (2013) strengthen this finding by applying both revealed preference (defensive behavior, $87 per symptom day) and stated preference (contingent valuation, $95 per symptom day) methods to the 2009 Station Fire. The convergence of two independent WTP estimation methods on nearly identical values -- and their dramatic divergence from COI estimates of $3 (traditional) and $17 (comprehensive) -- provides strong evidence that the WTP-COI gap is robust rather than an artifact of any single valuation approach.

The Viscusi and Aldy (2003) meta-analysis of over 60 studies on the value of a statistical life (VSL) establishes the broader empirical foundation for preference-based valuation of health risks. While focused on mortality, their finding that revealed-preference estimates from labor markets consistently yield large values for statistical lives underscores why WTP-based measures tend to be substantially larger than accounting-based approaches like COI. Robinson and Hammitt (2016) review more recent evidence on fatal illness risk valuation, finding that quality studies yield values comparable to the injury-based literature and reinforcing the case for WTP-based approaches in environmental health valuation.

Beyond the fundamental COI-WTP gap, several studies document specific categories of cost that BenMAP-CE's COI framework misses. Birnbaum et al. (2020) use insurance claims data to show that BenMAP-CE underestimates healthcare costs by approximately 40% because it captures only hospital admissions and ED visits while excluding ambulatory care -- physician office visits, prescriptions, and home health services. They find that every hospitalized respiratory patient averages five additional ambulatory visits not counted by the tool. This underestimation exists even within the COI framework before any WTP adjustment is considered.

Meng et al. (2024) focus specifically on work-loss days attributable to wildfire PM2.5 in California, using linked CHIS survey and air quality data. They find that wildfire smoke contributes 0.7--2.6 million work-loss days annually in California with economic losses of $129--521 million, and that BenMAP-CE underestimates this component by a factor of 5--10. The California-specific setting and wildfire-specific exposure measurement make this finding particularly relevant to the present analysis.

Han et al. (2024) document yet another uncounted cost category: defensive spending. Using retail scanner data from 2006--2019, they find that wildfire smoke exposure significantly increases purchases of air purifiers, cold remedies, nasal products, and related health goods. These expenditures represent real welfare costs that individuals incur to reduce exposure and mitigate symptoms -- costs that are captured by WTP but entirely absent from COI. Their additional finding that defensive spending varies with social vulnerability connects the valuation methodology debate to environmental justice concerns.

Kochi et al. (2016) provide a direct COI-based estimate of wildfire smoke ED visit costs for comparison. Their analysis of the 2007 Southern California wildfires estimates approximately 760 excess respiratory ED visits and 38 cardiovascular ED visits, with total associated medical costs exceeding $3.4 million. This figure, based purely on medical charges, illustrates how COI-only estimates can appear modest relative to the scale of the underlying health burden.

### Theme 3: Wildfire Smoke Epidemiology -- Establishing the Health Burden

The epidemiological evidence linking wildfire smoke to acute health outcomes is now mature, with consistent findings across multiple study designs, geographic contexts, and health endpoints.

Chen et al. (2023), conducted by OEHHA/CalEPA researchers, provide the foundational epidemiological estimates for this capstone. Using a time-stratified case-crossover design with California OSHPD ED visit data, they find that smoke events (air basin-days with wildfire PM2.5 at or above the 98th percentile, 13.5 ug/m3) are associated with a 14.4% increase in respiratory ED visits at lag 1, a 57.1% increase for asthma at lag 0, and a 12.7% increase for chronic lower respiratory disease at lag 0. The study covers 2016--2019, a period that includes the catastrophic 2017 and 2018 California fire seasons.

Wettstein et al. (2018) analyze over one million ED visits across eight California air basins during the 2015 wildfire season, extending the health burden beyond respiratory endpoints. They document elevated cardiovascular and cerebrovascular ED visits on all smoke days, with the greatest increases on dense smoke days among adults aged 65 and older. Specific elevations are observed for myocardial infarction, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, dysrhythmia, and stroke. This expansion of the endpoint scope has direct implications for economic valuation: restricting cost estimates to respiratory outcomes alone misses a substantial portion of the burden.

Two important findings complicate the standard linear dose-response assumptions embedded in BenMAP-CE. Heft-Neal et al. (2023) reveal that ED visits respond nonlinearly to wildfire smoke. Total ED visits actually decline at extreme smoke concentrations as people avoid leaving their homes, while respiratory-specific visits increase by 30--110%. This concave relationship means that linear extrapolation may mischaracterize the marginal health impact at high concentrations. From an economic perspective, the behavioral avoidance itself represents a welfare cost -- foregone activities, disrupted daily life -- that neither COI nor standard health impact assessments capture.

Aguilera et al. (2021) demonstrate that wildfire-origin PM2.5 is substantially more harmful per unit concentration than PM2.5 from other sources, with wildfire PM2.5 causing 1.3--10% increases in respiratory hospitalizations per 10 ug/m3 compared to only 0.67--1.3% for non-wildfire PM2.5. This differential toxicity factor of approximately 1.5--8x has major implications for BenMAP-CE, whose default concentration-response functions do not distinguish between PM2.5 sources. Darling et al. (2023) confirm this at the zip-code level across California for 2006--2019, finding that ignoring differential toxicity underestimates the respiratory health burden by approximately 13%, with greater underestimation in high social vulnerability index (SVI) areas.

The broader evidence base is synthesized in several review articles. Reid et al. (2016) provide a widely cited critical review documenting consistent respiratory morbidity associations, growing cardiovascular evidence, and important gaps regarding mental health effects and susceptible populations. Liu et al. (2015) offer a systematic review of non-occupational wildfire smoke health impacts, and Cascio (2018) adds the EPA perspective, emphasizing compounding risk factors of increasing fire frequency, expanding wildland-urban interface, and aging populations.

Liu et al. (2017), examining 561 Western U.S. counties from 2004--2009, find a 7.2% increase in respiratory hospital admissions during severe smoke wave days for Medicare enrollees, with no association at lower concentrations, suggesting a threshold effect that further challenges linear dose-response assumptions. Miller et al. (2024), in an NBER working paper using Medicare administrative records for 2007--2019, estimate that wildfire smoke accounts for 18% of ambient PM2.5, 0.42% of deaths, and 0.69% of ER visits among adults 65+. Critically, they find concave concentration-response relationships consistent with Heft-Neal et al. (2023), where smaller pollution shocks have outsized per-unit health impacts.

### Theme 4: Broader Economic Valuation of Air Pollution Health Effects

The capstone's analysis of wildfire smoke ED visit costs sits within a broader literature on the economic valuation of air pollution health effects, which provides methodological context and scale comparisons.

Deryugina et al. (2019), published in the American Economic Review, provide the gold standard for causal identification of air pollution health costs. Using wind direction as an instrument for PM2.5 exposure among elderly Medicare beneficiaries, they estimate causal effects on mortality, healthcare utilization, and medical costs. While focused on general PM2.5 rather than wildfire-specific exposure, their study establishes the credibility of large economic cost estimates for air pollution health impacts and demonstrates the importance of rigorous causal identification.

Fann et al. (2012) estimate the national public health burden from ambient PM2.5 and ozone exposure, finding approximately 130,000 PM2.5-related deaths from 2005 air quality levels. Their BenMAP-CE-based approach demonstrates the tool's application at national scale and provides context for understanding where wildfire-specific costs fit within the total air pollution burden.

Connolly et al. (2024) estimate 52,480--55,710 premature deaths attributable to wildland fire PM2.5 in California from 2008 to 2018, valued at $432--456 billion. This mortality estimate, derived using wildfire-specific dose-response coefficients, dwarfs any existing morbidity estimate and demonstrates two important points: first, that mortality dominates the total economic burden of wildfire smoke; and second, that applying wildfire-specific (rather than generic) dose-response functions substantially changes the results.

Qiu et al. (2026) develop an integrated framework combining econometric smoke-mortality relationships with climate-smoke projections, estimating that 3 degrees Celsius of future warming will lead to 46,200 annual smoke-related deaths in the U.S. and a partial social cost of carbon of $15.1 per tonne from wildfire smoke mortality alone. While focused on mortality and climate policy rather than morbidity valuation, this study underscores the growing economic significance of wildfire smoke health impacts and the importance of using accurate valuation methods.

Rappold et al. (2017) develop a Community Health-Vulnerability Index (CHVI) incorporating asthma prevalence, COPD, hypertension, diabetes, obesity, elderly population share, and socioeconomic indicators. While not an economic valuation study per se, their vulnerability framework is relevant for understanding why uniform per-visit unit values in BenMAP-CE may not adequately capture the differential economic burden across communities with varying baseline health conditions and socioeconomic resources.

### Theme 5: Environmental Justice Dimensions

The environmental justice literature provides critical context for evaluating BenMAP-CE's valuation approach, because if the tool's underestimation is systematically worse for vulnerable communities, then policy decisions based on BenMAP-CE outputs will undervalue protections for those who need them most.

Masri et al. (2021) examine California census tracts from 2000--2020 and find that areas with higher fire frequency and burned area have higher proportions of elderly and low-income residents, lower median household incomes, and greater proportions of Native American populations. Wildfire impacts in California are not distributed equally, and the populations most exposed are often those least equipped to bear the economic costs.

At the broader air pollution level, Liu et al. (2021) find that for all years and all six criteria pollutants studied, the racial/ethnic group with the highest national average exposure is always a minority group. For PM2.5 in 2010, non-Hispanic Black populations have exposures at least 5% above average in 63% of states, with absolute exposure disparities 1.1--21 times larger across racial/ethnic groups than across income categories. Tessum et al. (2021) demonstrate that nearly all major emission categories disproportionately expose people of color to PM2.5 -- a systemic pattern rather than an incidental one. Tessum et al. (2019) trace this disparity to its structural roots, finding that non-Hispanic whites experience approximately 17% less PM2.5 exposure than caused by their consumption, while Black and Hispanic populations bear approximately 56% and 63% excess exposure, respectively.

Darling et al. (2023) bridge the epidemiology and environmental justice strands directly. Their zip-code-level analysis for California finds that ignoring the differential toxicity of wildfire PM2.5 underestimates the respiratory health burden more severely in high social vulnerability index areas. This finding implies that BenMAP-CE's use of generic PM2.5 concentration-response functions not only underestimates the overall health burden but does so in a way that is systematically biased against the most vulnerable communities.

Han et al. (2024) add that defensive spending -- the purchase of air purifiers, medications, and other protective goods -- varies significantly with social vulnerability. Communities with fewer resources spend less on defensive measures, not because they face less risk, but because they have less capacity to respond. This means that WTP measures derived from general population averages may themselves underestimate the welfare losses in low-income and minority communities, even as they represent a substantial improvement over COI.

## Gaps and Opportunities

1. **No integrated valuation critique for wildfire smoke ED visits.** Individual papers have identified specific dimensions of BenMAP-CE underestimation -- the WTP-COI gap (Richardson et al. 2012, 2013), ambulatory care exclusion (Birnbaum et al. 2020), work-loss undercount (Meng et al. 2024), differential toxicity (Aguilera et al. 2021), and defensive spending (Han et al. 2024) -- but no existing study has assembled these critiques into a comprehensive assessment for a single health endpoint in a single state. This is the primary gap that the capstone addresses.

2. **California-specific morbidity valuation is missing.** Connolly et al. (2024) provide California-specific wildfire mortality valuation ($432--456 billion), but no equivalent California-specific morbidity valuation exists for ED visits. Chen et al. (2023) provide the epidemiology; the economic valuation has not been done.

3. **Disconnect between epidemiology and economics.** Studies either estimate ED visit counts (Chen et al. 2023; Heft-Neal et al. 2023) or critique valuation methods (Richardson et al. 2012, 2013; Birnbaum et al. 2020) but do not combine state-of-the-art epidemiological estimates with WTP-adjusted unit values for a concrete policy application.

4. **Equity-adjusted valuation is unexplored.** While the environmental justice literature documents unequal exposure and vulnerability, no study examines whether BenMAP-CE's uniform unit values per ED visit capture the differential economic burden across communities with different baseline health conditions, income levels, and adaptive capacities.

5. **Behavioral avoidance costs are unquantified.** Heft-Neal et al. (2023) document that people change their behavior to avoid smoke exposure, and Han et al. (2024) measure one dimension of this (defensive spending), but the total welfare cost of behavioral avoidance -- including foregone outdoor activities, disrupted work and school, and psychological distress -- has not been incorporated into any wildfire smoke valuation framework.

6. **Stieb et al. WTP estimates are absent from this review.** The research question references Stieb et al. as a potential WTP-based alternative to BenMAP-CE's COI values, but this reference is not present in the bibliography. If Stieb et al. provide ED-visit-specific WTP estimates, they would be an important addition to the WTP evidence base alongside Richardson et al. This gap should be addressed by locating and incorporating the Stieb et al. reference.

7. **Interaction effects among underestimation dimensions.** The individual adjustment factors (9x WTP-COI ratio, 40% ambulatory gap, 5--10x work-loss undercount) were estimated independently in different contexts. How they interact when applied simultaneously is unknown. Some overlap is likely -- for example, ambulatory care costs may be partially captured in the WTP estimate -- and a transparent treatment of interaction effects is needed.

## Suggested Next Steps

- **Locate and incorporate Stieb et al.** The research question references Stieb et al. as providing WTP-based ED visit valuations. Find the specific publication, determine whether it offers a direct WTP estimate for ED visits that could serve as an alternative to BenMAP-CE's COI values, and add it to the bibliography.

- **Extract and tabulate BenMAP-CE's specific default unit values for ED visits from Appendix H.** The capstone requires the exact dollar figures BenMAP-CE assigns to respiratory and cardiovascular ED visits, including the underlying studies from which they are derived and the year dollars.

- **Construct a scenario analysis framework.** Rather than applying all adjustment factors simultaneously (which risks double-counting), develop a range of scenarios: (a) BenMAP-CE baseline (COI only), (b) COI + ambulatory care adjustment (Birnbaum et al.), (c) full WTP replacement using Richardson et al. ratios, (d) comprehensive adjustment incorporating multiple dimensions with explicit treatment of interaction effects.

- **Determine whether Chen et al. (2023) provide sufficient detail to calculate excess ED visit counts.** The capstone needs to convert the reported percent increases in risk (e.g., 14.4% for respiratory ED visits) into absolute numbers of excess visits. This requires baseline ED visit counts by category, which may be in the Chen et al. supplementary materials or in the California OSHPD data.

- **Consider the temporal and geographic transferability of Richardson et al.'s WTP estimates.** The 9:1 WTP-COI ratio comes from 2009 data for a specific Southern California fire. Evaluate whether this ratio should be adjusted for inflation, for statewide (vs. urban Los Angeles) application, or for the 2016--2019 study period.

- **Engage with the nonlinearity evidence.** Heft-Neal et al. (2023) and Miller et al. (2024) both find concave dose-response relationships. Consider whether the economic valuation should account for this nonlinearity, either by adjusting the per-visit valuation at different smoke intensity levels or by discussing it as a limitation of the linear approach.
