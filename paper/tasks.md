# Literature Review Tasks

Each drafting task corresponds to exactly one paragraph in the final paper. Non-drafting tasks are listed at the end.

---

## Section I: Motivation (3 paragraphs)

**Task 1** | Section I, paragraph 1
Establish that wildfire smoke is a growing and quantifiable public health threat in California, with Chen et al. (2023) documenting 4,597 respiratory and 889 cardiovascular smoke-attributable ED visits over four years (2016-2019).
Sources: Chen et al. (2023); Reid et al. (2016) for background on growing fire seasons; Aguilera et al. (2021) for differential toxicity as additional motivation.

**Task 2** | Section I, paragraph 2
Explain that policymakers need dollar values for these health outcomes to justify wildfire management spending, and that BenMAP-CE is EPA's standard tool for producing those values. Introduce the question: is BenMAP using the most appropriate economic method, and what alternatives does the literature offer?
Sources: EPA Guidelines (2024); BenMAP-CE User's Manual and Appendix H; Dittrich and McCallum (2020) for the point that BenMAP is the dominant tool in this literature.

**Task 3** | Section I, paragraph 3 (thesis paragraph)
State the thesis: BenMAP-CE uses cost-of-illness to value ED visits, which economic theory and empirical evidence show is an underestimate of the true welfare cost. This review examines how willingness-to-pay approaches and more complete measures of illness cost in the wildfire smoke literature could improve BenMAP-CE's ED visit valuations.
Sources: Harrington and Portney (1987) for the theoretical lower-bound argument; EPA Guidelines (2024) for the institutional context.

## Section II: Background on Economic Methods (2 paragraphs)

**Task 4** | Section II, paragraph 1
Define the three main approaches economists use to value health outcomes: cost of illness (medical costs plus lost earnings), willingness to pay (revealed or stated preference), and benefit transfer. Explain how each differs in data demands and theoretical validity.
Sources: Textbook; Harrington and Portney (1987) for COI definition and omissions; EPA Guidelines (2024) for the typology; Viscusi and Aldy (2003) for revealed-preference background.

**Task 5** | Section II, paragraph 2
Explain that COI is a theoretical lower bound on WTP because it omits pain/suffering, defensive expenditures, and risk aversion. Note that EPA guidelines prefer WTP in principle, but COI dominates in practice because few WTP estimates exist for morbidity endpoints. Add that the size of the COI-WTP gap varies by endpoint: Van Houtven et al. (2006) find WTP increases far more steeply with severity (elasticity ~2.0) than with duration (~0.5), which means the gap is especially large for short-duration, high-severity events like ED visits.
Sources: Harrington and Portney (1987); EPA Guidelines (2024); Robinson and Hammitt (2016) for the broader valuation hierarchy; Van Houtven et al. (2006) for the severity-duration asymmetry.

## Section III: How BenMAP-CE Fits In (2 paragraphs)

**Task 6** | Section III, paragraph 1
Explain BenMAP-CE's four-stage pipeline (air quality modeling, concentration-response functions, health impact counts, dollar valuation) and that for ED visits it uses COI only, producing $875/respiratory and $1,161/cardiovascular ED visit in 2015 USD from HCUP NEDS data adjusted by NIS cost-to-charge ratios.
Sources: BenMAP-CE Appendix H; EPA (2023) User's Manual.

**Task 7** | Section III, paragraph 2
Give balanced treatment of BenMAP's strengths and limitations. Strengths: transparency, reproducibility, national representativeness, scalability to any ICD-coded endpoint, and conservatism (unlikely to overstate costs). Limitations: omits welfare components beyond medical costs, uses generic rather than wildfire-specific concentration-response functions, misses ambulatory care costs (~40%), and underestimates work-loss costs (5-10x for wildfire). Present these as design tradeoffs rather than failures.
Sources: BenMAP-CE Appendix H for strengths; Aguilera et al. (2021) for wildfire-specific CRFs; Birnbaum et al. (2020) for ambulatory gap; Meng et al. (2024) for work-loss underestimate; EPA Guidelines (2024) for the COI-lower-bound acknowledgment.

## Section IV: Other Studies Valuing Wildfire Smoke Health Outcomes

**Task 8** | Section IV, transition paragraph
Bridge from Section III to the empirical literature. Restate that BenMAP has specific limitations and explain that this section examines how researchers have valued wildfire smoke health outcomes using both COI and WTP methods.
Sources: No new sources. This paragraph connects Sections III and IV.

### IV.A: COI-Based Wildfire Studies (2 paragraphs)

**Task 9** | Section IV.A, paragraph 1
Synthesize the four major COI-based wildfire studies: Kochi et al. (2016) found $3.4M from the 2007 SoCal fires, Jones (2016) showed that wildfire-specific CRFs substantially increase estimates, Jones and Berrens (2017) estimated $165M/year across the Western US with 217% annual growth, and Fann et al. (2018) produced the most comprehensive national assessment.
Sources: Kochi et al. (2016); Jones (2016); Jones and Berrens (2017); Fann et al. (2018); Connolly et al. (2024) for the mortality-cost contrast that makes $3.4M appear negligible.

**Task 10** | Section IV.A, paragraph 2
Evaluate COI-based approaches as a group. They are highly scalable and data-driven, which explains their dominance in regulatory settings. Their limitation is that they can only be as comprehensive as the unit values they plug in, so improvements to BenMAP's unit values would flow through to every study that uses the tool.
Sources: Dittrich and McCallum (2020) for the systematic review perspective; the four studies from Task 9 as evidence of the pattern.

### IV.B: WTP-Based Wildfire Studies (3 paragraphs)

**Task 11** | Section IV.B, paragraph 1
Present Richardson et al. (2012, 2013) as the key wildfire-specific WTP studies. Defensive-behavior WTP of $84-87/day and stated-preference WTP of $95/day converge with each other but diverge substantially from COI ($3-17/day), a gap of 5x to 29x depending on the COI denominator.
Sources: Richardson et al. (2012); Richardson et al. (2013).

**Task 12** | Section IV.B, paragraph 2
Present Stieb et al. (2002) as the most directly substitutable alternative to BenMAP's unit values. Explain the component framework (V_T = V_COT + V_LP + V_SP), the Johnson et al. (2000) stated-preference survey, the Saint John ED follow-up data, and the resulting values of $2,752/respiratory and $6,054/cardiovascular ED visit in 2015 USD, which are 3.1x and 5.2x BenMAP's values.
Sources: Stieb et al. (2002); Johnson et al. (2000) as the underlying WTP survey.

**Task 13** | Section IV.B, paragraph 3
Evaluate WTP-based approaches as a group. Richardson's two-method convergence is compelling, and Stieb's component framework offers a structured way to separate cost categories. However, practical barriers remain: Richardson's values are per symptom-day (not per ED visit), requiring conversion assumptions, and Stieb's underlying data is from 1990s Canada with a different healthcare system. Discuss these as tradeoffs against accuracy rather than disqualifying flaws.
Sources: Richardson et al. (2012, 2013) for the unit mismatch; Stieb et al. (2002) for the geographic transferability issue.

### IV.C: Studies Documenting Specific Gaps in BenMAP (2 paragraphs)

**Task 14** | Section IV.C, paragraph 1
Explain that these studies quantify specific cost categories BenMAP omits, independent of the broader COI-vs-WTP debate. Cover Birnbaum et al. (2020) on the ambulatory care gap (~40%, with every hospitalized respiratory patient averaging five additional uncounted visits) and Meng et al. (2024) on the work-loss underestimate (5-10x for wildfire in California, 0.7-2.6M work-loss days/year).
Sources: Birnbaum et al. (2020); Meng et al. (2024).

**Task 15** | Section IV.C, paragraph 2
Cover defensive spending (Han et al. 2024) and behavioral avoidance (Heft-Neal et al. 2023) as welfare costs captured by neither COI nor standard epidemiological counts. Note that defensive spending varies by social vulnerability, meaning the uncounted costs fall disproportionately on disadvantaged communities.
Sources: Han et al. (2024); Heft-Neal et al. (2023); Masri et al. (2021) and Darling et al. (2023) for the equity dimension.

### IV.D: Which Outcomes Carry the Biggest Economic Costs (2 paragraphs)

**Task 16** | Section IV.D, paragraph 1
Compare the scale of different health outcomes. Mortality dominates total wildfire health costs ($432-456B in California over 2008-2018). Among morbidity endpoints, work-loss days affect far more people than ED visits (0.7-2.6M days/year vs. ~5,500 attributable ED visits over four years). Morbidity valuation is a complement to mortality valuation, not a substitute.
Sources: Connolly et al. (2024) for mortality costs; Meng et al. (2024) for work-loss scale; Chen et al. (2023) for ED visit counts.

**Task 17** | Section IV.D, paragraph 2
Compare respiratory versus cardiovascular ED visit costs under both COI and WTP. Address the scalability question: COI-based BenMAP approaches and Stieb-style component frameworks can extend to many cases, while Richardson-style WTP studies require expensive site-specific data collection.
Sources: Chen et al. (2023) for respiratory vs. cardiovascular counts; BenMAP Appendix H for COI values; Stieb et al. (2002) for WTP values; Richardson et al. (2012, 2013) for the scalability contrast.

## Section V: Recommendations (3 paragraphs)

**Task 18** | Section V, paragraph 1
Describe the ideal BenMAP with unlimited resources: WTP-based unit values from large-scale preference studies designed for wildfire smoke ED visits, wildfire-specific concentration-response functions, full welfare accounting, and equity weighting.
Sources: Aguilera et al. (2021) for wildfire-specific CRFs; Masri et al. (2021) and Darling et al. (2023) for equity weighting; EPA Guidelines (2024) for the WTP preference.

**Task 19** | Section V, paragraph 2
Describe what could be done with moderate new data collection: update Stieb's framework with a modern US WTP survey analogous to Johnson et al. (2000), apply Richardson's defensive-behavior method to a broader sample of California fires, and link HCUP to CHIS for work-loss and out-of-pocket cost estimation.
Sources: Stieb et al. (2002); Johnson et al. (2000); Richardson et al. (2012, 2013); Meng et al. (2024) for the CHIS linkage.

**Task 20** | Section V, paragraph 3
Describe what can be done now with existing public data: apply Stieb's converted WTP values alongside BenMAP's COI values to report a range, add Birnbaum's 40% ambulatory care adjustment, and apply Richardson's conservative 5x wildfire calibration factor. Explain that 5x uses the most generous COI denominator ($87/$17) and is the lower bound of a 5x-29x range, yet still implies BenMAP captures at most 20% of welfare cost.
Sources: Stieb et al. (2002); Birnbaum et al. (2020); Richardson et al. (2013) for the 5x-29x range.

## Section VI: Conclusion (2 paragraphs)

**Task 21** | Section VI, paragraph 1
Restate that BenMAP uses COI, which is well-suited to scalable regulatory analysis but captures a narrow slice of welfare cost for ED visits. The wildfire smoke literature offers alternatives at different points on the accuracy-scalability tradeoff, and the most immediately feasible improvements involve pairing BenMAP's COI values with published WTP adjustments to report a cost range rather than a single number.
Sources: Richardson et al. (2013) for the 89%/5% statistic (echoing the thesis); BenMAP Appendix H for what it does capture; Stieb et al. (2002) for the substitutable alternative.

**Task 22** | Section VI, paragraph 2
Acknowledge that health costs are only one category of wildfire economic costs. Property damage, suppression costs, lost tourism, ecosystem services, and productivity effects are all substantial and outside this review's scope, so even the WTP-adjusted estimates presented here are a fraction of total wildfire economic burden.
Sources: Connolly et al. (2024) for scale context. No deep citation needed; this is a scoping disclaimer.
