Wildfire smoke is a growing public health threat in California. Toxicological evidence suggests that fine particulate matter (PM2.5) created by wildfires is more harmful than ambient PM2.5, producing a higher risk of emergency department (ED) visits (Wegesser et al. 2009; Elser et al. 2023). Chen et al. (2023) estimated this threat for California by running regressions for each air basin and combining them through a random effects meta analysis. They estimated that wildfire smoke was associated with a 14.3% increase in the risk of respiratory ED visits and a 3.2% increase in the risk of cardiovascular ED visits. Across California, these risk estimates suggest that 4,597 respiratory and 889 cardiovascular ED visits were caused by wildfire smoke from 2016 and 2019\. The cost of those visits, however, depends on how it is measured. The EPA’s Benefits Mapping and Analysis Program (BenMAP) values an ED visit using only hospital medical costs, a method known as cost of illness. But the EPA itself acknowledges that the cost of illness is an underestimate, because it omits what people would be willing to pay to avoid the illness altogether: lost productivity, pain and suffering, and averting expenditures. This paper estimates the economic impact of ED visits caused by wildfire smoke in California under both approaches and finds that the cost of illness method underestimates the total economic burden by about a factor of three.  
	Chen et al. (2023) divided California into 15 air basins and estimated a separate regression in each basin. They measured how days with wildfire smoke affected the number of respiratory and cardiovascular ED visits. Four basins experienced no wildfire events during the study period and were removed. The remaining basins were combined through a random effects meta analysis. The pooled risk increases were 14.3% for respiratory ED visits and 3.2% for cardiovascular ED visits. To translate these percentages into ED visit counts, Chen et al. applied a health impact function that multiplied each basin’s baseline ED visit rate, population, and number of wildfire smoke days with the estimated risk coefficients. The result was 4,597 respiratory and 889 cardiovascular ED visits attributable to wildfire smoke in California from 2016 to 2019\. These are the counts that the rest of the paper assigns dollar values to.  
BenMAP is the tool that assigns dollar values to those ED visit counts. It estimates a single cost of illness figure per visit by combining two datasets from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP). The Nationwide Emergency Department Sample (NEDS), contains individual ED discharge records. Each record includes a diagnosis code, billed charges, and a discharge weight reflecting how many similar visits it represents nationally. The EPA filters NEDS to respiratory or cardiovascular diagnoses and computes a weighted national mean of billed charges for each category. However, billed charges are not the actual costs but list prices. To convert them, EPA turns to the National Inpatient Sample (NIS), which reports hospital cost to charge ratios (CCR). A CCR is the ratio of a hospital’s actual costs to its billed charges. The EPA averages the CCRs across hospitals matching the same diagnosis codes and applies that single ratio to the NEDS mean. The resulting values are $875 per respiratory ED visit and $1,161 per cardiovascular ED visit (2015 USD). These figures capture medical costs alone. They do not include lost wages during illness, pain and suffering, and averting expenditures.   
	However, there are advantages to using the cost of illness method. Its values are derived from real hospital bill charges, and the method is transparent and replicable. Any research can pull the same HCUP datasets, apply the same filters, and arrive at the same figures. In contrast, willingness to pay surveys rely on stated preference surveys where respondents can overstate payments they will never actually face. Moreover, small changes in question wording can shift the results substantially. The cost of illness method sidesteps these problems by grounding its estimates in records. There are strengths to the cost of illness method, but it is not clear whether medical costs alone capture the total economic burden of a wildfire smoke ED visit.   
Stieb et al. (2002) propose a framework that captures the total value of avoiding an ED visit. Their total valuation sums three components: cost of treatment, lost productivity, and a stated preference term that bundles pain and suffering with averting expenditures. Cost of treatment and lost productivity were estimated from ED records at a hospital in Saint John, New Brunswick. The stated preference component drew on a willingness to pay model developed by Johnson et al. (2000), which used surveys presenting healthy participants with varying symptom combinations, activity restrictions, and illness durations. Stieb et al. matched those combinations to the clinical composition of their ED sample, weighted each by its observed prevalence, and summed the results. The framework produced total values of CAN$2,000 per respiratory visit and CAN$4,400 per cardiovascular visit in 1997 Canadian dollars. Converted to 2015 USD to match BenMAP’s dollar year, these become $2,752 and $6,054. Compared to BenMAP’s COI estimates of $875 and $1,161, the Stieb et al. values are 3.1 and 5.2 times larger. The gap is large because pain and suffering alone can rival the cost of treatment. For respiratory visits, the stated preference component accounts for roughly half of the total value. A valuation method that excludes pain and suffering therefore misses at least half of what an avoided ED visit is actually worth.
