# Annotated Bibliography: Economic Valuation of Wildfire Smoke-Attributable ED Visits in California

**Capstone Research Question:** What is the economic impact of wildfire smoke-attributable ED visits in California, and are BenMAP-CE's COI unit values the most appropriate?

**Proximity Scale:**
- 5 = Directly addresses the research question (same question + same/similar data)
- 4 = Closely related, different angle or context
- 3 = Related method or related context
- 2 = Tangentially relevant
- 1 = Background/foundational

---

## Category 1: BenMAP-CE and EPA Valuation Framework

### U.S. EPA (2023). *Environmental Benefits Mapping and Analysis Program -- Community Edition (BenMAP-CE) User's Manual: Appendices.* Version 1.5.11, April 2023.
The BenMAP-CE User's Manual and its appendices (particularly Appendix H on health valuation functions) constitute the primary technical documentation for the tool at the center of this capstone. Appendix H specifies the default unit cost values for emergency department visits, hospital admissions, and other health endpoints that BenMAP-CE uses to monetize air pollution health impacts. Understanding these default values -- their derivation, underlying assumptions, and limitations -- is essential for evaluating whether they adequately capture the true economic burden of wildfire smoke-attributable ED visits.
**Proximity: 5**

### U.S. EPA (2024). *Guidelines for Preparing Economic Analyses.* 3rd Edition, EPA-240-R-24-001. Washington, DC.
The EPA's updated guidelines provide the official methodological framework for valuing health endpoints in regulatory benefit-cost analyses. Chapter 7 addresses valuation of health effects including morbidity, distinguishing between cost-of-illness (COI), averting behavior, and stated preference (WTP) approaches. The guidelines acknowledge that relatively few WTP estimates exist for many morbidity risks, making COI the default in many applications despite its known tendency to underestimate true welfare losses. This document establishes the institutional context for why BenMAP-CE relies on COI-based unit values.
**Proximity: 4**

### Jones, B.A. (2016). Wildfire smoke health costs: A methods case study for a Southwestern US 'mega-fire'. *Journal of Environmental Economics and Policy*, 5(2), 181--199.
Jones applies BenMAP-CE to estimate the health costs of smoke exposure from the 2011 Wallow Fire in Arizona/New Mexico, finding a per capita WTP of $130 (2014 dollars) to avoid wildfire smoke health effects in Albuquerque. The study demonstrates that transferring wildfire-specific concentration-response functions into BenMAP-CE produces substantially higher morbidity estimates and costs compared to using the tool's default urban air pollution studies. This is directly relevant because it demonstrates that BenMAP-CE's default parameters may be inappropriate for wildfire contexts.
**Proximity: 5**

### Jones, B.A., & Berrens, R.P. (2017). Application of an original wildfire smoke health cost benefits transfer protocol to the Western US, 2005--2015. *Environmental Management*, 60(5), 809--822.
The authors develop and apply a benefits transfer protocol for estimating wildfire smoke health costs across the entire Western US, finding average annual morbidity and mortality costs of $165 million. They estimate wildfire smoke costs grew by 217% annually between 2010 and 2015. The study fills a gap by providing the first time-series estimates of PM2.5 smoke costs across mortality and morbidity measures for the Western US, and the benefits transfer methodology is relevant to this capstone's approach of applying unit values to epidemiological estimates.
**Proximity: 4**

### Fann, N., Alman, B., Broome, R.A., Morgan, G.G., Johnston, F.H., Pouliot, G., & Rappold, A.G. (2018). The health impacts and economic value of wildland fire episodes in the U.S.: 2008--2012. *Science of the Total Environment*, 610--611, 802--809.
This study provides a national-scale assessment of health impacts from wildland fire episodes using CMAQ-modeled fire PM2.5, CDC WONDER data, and BenMAP-CE for economic valuation. It covers cardiovascular, respiratory, and other outcomes across 2008--2012. As the most comprehensive national application of BenMAP-CE to wildfire health valuation, it serves as a direct methodological precedent for this capstone, though its national scope differs from the California focus.
**Proximity: 4**

### Dittrich, R., & McCallum, S. (2020). How to measure the economic health cost of wildfires -- A systematic review of the literature for northern America. *International Journal of Wildland Fire*, 29(11), 961--973.
This systematic review examines the literature on economic health costs of wildfire smoke since 2006, focusing on key methodological choices including cost estimators for mortality and morbidity effects. The authors provide an in-depth discussion of BenMAP-CE and guidance on its application, concluding that all studies find highly significant health costs in the millions or billions of dollars, often driven by mortality. The review is valuable for contextualizing the choice of valuation approach in this capstone and identifying methodological best practices.
**Proximity: 4**

## Category 2: COI vs. WTP Methodology

### Harrington, W. & Portney, P.R. (1987). Valuing the benefits of health and safety regulation. *Journal of Urban Economics*, 22(1), 101--112.
This seminal paper provides the theoretical foundation for understanding why cost-of-illness (COI) is a lower bound on willingness to pay (WTP) for health risk reductions. Harrington and Portney demonstrate that COI captures only direct medical costs and lost earnings, systematically omitting pain and suffering, defensive expenditures, and risk aversion -- welfare components that can be substantial. The paper was produced under contract with EPA's Office of Air Quality Planning and Standards. This is the theoretical backbone for the capstone's central argument that BenMAP-CE's reliance on COI-based unit values underestimates the true welfare costs of wildfire smoke-attributable ED visits.
**Proximity: 5**

### Viscusi, W.K. & Aldy, J.E. (2003). The value of a statistical life: A critical review of market estimates throughout the world. *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty*, 27(1), 5--76.
This comprehensive review synthesizes over 60 studies of mortality risk premiums from ten countries and approximately 40 studies of injury risk premiums, establishing the empirical basis for the value of a statistical life (VSL) framework. The meta-analysis finds an income elasticity of VSL of approximately 0.5--0.6. The VSL framework underpins EPA's valuation hierarchy where WTP-based measures are preferred over COI, and this paper documents the extensive revealed-preference evidence base that supports that hierarchy. Understanding the VSL literature is essential context for why EPA guidelines acknowledge COI as a second-best approach when WTP estimates are unavailable -- the precise situation BenMAP-CE faces for many morbidity endpoints including ED visits.
**Proximity: 4**

### Richardson, L.A., Champ, P.A., & Loomis, J.B. (2012). The hidden cost of wildfires: Economic valuation of health effects of wildfire smoke exposure in Southern California. *Journal of Forest Economics*, 18(1), 14--35.
This foundational study estimates both COI ($9.50 per person per day) and WTP via the defensive behavior method ($84.42 per person per day) for wildfire smoke exposure during a major Los Angeles County wildfire. The nearly 9x gap between WTP and COI demonstrates that COI substantially underestimates the true welfare cost because it ignores defensive expenditures and disutility. This is perhaps the single most important methodological reference for this capstone's central argument about BenMAP-CE's reliance on COI.
**Proximity: 5**

### Richardson, L.A., Loomis, J.B., & Champ, P.A. (2013). Valuing morbidity from wildfire smoke exposure: A comparison of revealed and stated preference techniques. *Land Economics*, 89(1), 76--100.
Building on their 2012 study, the authors compare revealed preference (defensive behavior) and stated preference (contingent valuation) WTP estimates for wildfire smoke morbidity using data from the 2009 Station Fire. WTP estimates of $87 (defensive behavior) and $95 (contingent valuation) per symptom day are not statistically different from each other but differ dramatically from COI estimates of $3 (traditional) and $17 (comprehensive). This convergence of two WTP methods strengthens the case that COI-based valuations used in BenMAP-CE are substantial underestimates.
**Proximity: 5**

### Birnbaum, H.G., Carley, C.D., Desai, U., Ou, S., & Zuckerman, P.R. (2020). Measuring the impact of air pollution on health care costs. *Health Affairs*, 39(12), 2113--2119.
Using real-world claims data, the authors find that BenMAP-CE's default configuration underestimates healthcare costs by approximately 40% because it captures only hospital admissions and ED visits while excluding ambulatory care (physician visits, prescriptions, home health). Every hospitalized respiratory patient averaged five additional ambulatory visits not counted by BenMAP-CE. This study provides direct empirical evidence of a specific gap in BenMAP-CE's cost accounting framework.
**Proximity: 5**

### Meng, Y.-Y., Rull, R.P., Gershunov, A., Hechtman, K., & Herman, P.M. (2024). Health and economic cost estimates of short-term total and wildfire PM2.5 exposure on work loss: Using the consecutive California Health Interview Survey (CHIS) data 2015--2018. *BMJ Public Health*, 2(1), e000649.
The authors link California Health Interview Survey respondents to daily PM2.5 and wildfire-specific PM2.5 data, finding that wildfire smoke contributes 0.7--2.6 million work-loss days annually in California with economic losses of $129--521 million. Critically, they find BenMAP-CE underestimates wildfire work-loss by 5--10x. Though focused on work-loss days rather than ED visits, this California-specific study using CHIS data directly supports the capstone's argument that BenMAP-CE undervalues wildfire smoke morbidity costs.
**Proximity: 5**

### Robinson, L.A., & Hammitt, J.K. (2016). Valuing reductions in fatal illness risks: Implications of recent research. *Health Economics*, 25(12), 1477--1491.
This review examines the evidence base for valuing mortality risk reductions (expressed as VSL), finding that few US studies of illness-related risks meet quality criteria, but those that do yield values similar to injury-based studies. While focused on mortality rather than morbidity, the methodological discussion of WTP-based valuation versus accounting-based approaches provides important theoretical grounding for why preference-based measures should be preferred in health valuation for environmental policy.
**Proximity: 3**

### Kochi, I., Champ, P.A., Loomis, J.B., & Donovan, G.H. (2016). Valuing morbidity effects of wildfire smoke exposure from the 2007 Southern California wildfires. *Journal of Forest Economics*, 25, 29--54.
Using data from 187 hospital facilities and 140 EDs in five Southern California counties during the 2007 wildfires, the authors estimate approximately 80 excess respiratory hospital admissions, 760 excess respiratory ED visits, 26 cardiovascular hospital admissions, and 38 cardiovascular ED visits, with total associated medical costs exceeding $3.4 million. This COI-based estimate of wildfire smoke ED visit costs in Southern California is a direct precedent for this capstone's analysis.
**Proximity: 5**

## Category 3: Wildfire Smoke Epidemiology

### Chen, A.I., Ebisu, K., Benmarhnia, T., & Basu, R. (2023). Emergency department visits associated with wildfire smoke events in California, 2016--2019. *Environmental Research*, 238(Pt 1), 117154.
This study examines associations between smoke events (air basin-days with wildfire PM2.5 at or above the 98th percentile, 13.5 ug/m3) and ED visits for respiratory, cardiovascular, diabetes, and mental health outcomes in California. Smoke events were associated with 14.4% increased risk of respiratory ED visits (lag 1), 57.1% increase for asthma (lag 0), and 12.7% for chronic lower respiratory disease (lag 0). Conducted by OEHHA/CalEPA researchers, this is the foundational epidemiological study that the capstone builds upon to estimate economic costs.
**Proximity: 5**

### Reid, C.E., Brauer, M., Johnston, F.H., Jerrett, M., Balmes, J.R., & Elliott, C.T. (2016). Critical review of health impacts of wildfire smoke exposure. *Environmental Health Perspectives*, 124(9), 1334--1343.
This widely cited review documents consistent evidence linking wildfire smoke exposure to respiratory morbidity, specifically asthma and COPD exacerbations, with growing evidence for all-cause mortality associations. The review identifies important gaps: uncertain cardiovascular associations, unknown susceptible populations, and limited evidence on mental health effects. It establishes the scientific consensus on wildfire smoke health effects that underpins the epidemiological estimates used in economic valuations.
**Proximity: 3**

### Aguilera, R., Corringham, T., Gershunov, A., & Benmarhnia, T. (2021). Wildfire smoke impacts respiratory health more than fine particles from other sources: Observational evidence from Southern California. *Nature Communications*, 12, 1493.
The authors find that wildfire PM2.5 causes 1.3--10% increases in respiratory hospitalizations per 10 ug/m3, compared to only 0.67--1.3% for non-wildfire PM2.5. This differential toxicity finding -- that wildfire smoke is approximately 1.5--8x more harmful per unit of PM2.5 -- has major implications for economic valuation because BenMAP-CE's default concentration-response functions do not distinguish between wildfire and non-wildfire PM2.5 sources. Using generic dose-response functions may underestimate health impacts and thus economic costs.
**Proximity: 4**

### Heft-Neal, S., Gould, C.F., Childs, M.L., Kiang, M.V., Nadeau, K.C., Duggan, M., Bendavid, E., & Burke, M. (2023). Emergency department visits respond nonlinearly to wildfire smoke. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 120(39), e2302409120.
This study reveals that total ED visits decline at extreme smoke concentrations due to behavioral avoidance (people staying indoors), while respiratory-specific visits increase by 30--110%. This nonlinear, concave dose-response relationship has important implications for economic valuation: linear extrapolation from epidemiological studies may misrepresent the marginal health impact at high smoke concentrations, and behavioral avoidance itself represents an uncounted welfare cost.
**Proximity: 4**

### Wettstein, Z.S., Hoshiko, S., Fahimi, J., Harrison, R.J., Cascio, W.E., & Rappold, A.G. (2018). Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular emergency department visits associated with wildfire smoke exposure in California in 2015. *Journal of the American Heart Association*, 7(8), e007492.
Analyzing over one million ED visits across eight California air basins during 2015 wildfires, the study finds elevated cardiovascular and cerebrovascular ED visits on all smoke days, with greatest increases on dense smoke days among adults 65+. Specific elevations include myocardial infarction, ischemic heart disease, heart failure, dysrhythmia, and stroke. This study expands the scope of wildfire smoke-attributable ED visits beyond respiratory endpoints, relevant to the capstone's comprehensive cost estimation.
**Proximity: 4**

### Liu, J.C., Pereira, G., Uhl, S.A., Bravo, M.A., & Bell, M.L. (2015). A systematic review of the physical health impacts from non-occupational exposure to wildfire smoke. *Environmental Research*, 136, 120--132.
This systematic review synthesizes evidence on non-occupational health effects of wildfire smoke exposure, confirming strong associations with respiratory morbidity and identifying emerging evidence for cardiovascular effects. The review provides the broader evidence base that supports including multiple health endpoints when valuing wildfire smoke ED visits, rather than limiting analysis to respiratory outcomes alone.
**Proximity: 2**

### Liu, J.C., Wilson, A., Mickley, L.J., Dominici, F., Ebisu, K., Wang, Y., Sulprizio, M.P., Peng, R.D., Yue, X., Son, J.-Y., Anderson, G.B., & Bell, M.L. (2017). Wildfire-specific fine particulate matter and risk of hospital admissions in urban and rural counties. *Epidemiology*, 28(1), 77--85.
Examining 561 Western US counties from 2004--2009, the study finds a 7.2% increase in respiratory hospital admissions during severe smoke wave days (wildfire PM2.5 > 37 ug/m3) for Medicare enrollees. No association was found at lower concentrations, suggesting a threshold effect. The study uses GEOS-Chem chemical transport modeling to isolate wildfire-specific PM2.5, a methodological approach also used in the concentration-response functions applied in BenMAP-CE.
**Proximity: 3**

### Cascio, W.E. (2018). Wildland fire smoke and human health. *Science of the Total Environment*, 624, 586--595.
This EPA-supported review confirms positive associations between wildfire smoke/PM2.5 exposure and all-cause mortality and respiratory morbidity while noting mixed evidence for cardiovascular outcomes. The review emphasizes that increasing fire frequency, expanding wildland-urban interface, and an aging population are compounding exposure risks. It identifies the need for research over wider geographic areas and longer time periods to better define risks for sensitive populations.
**Proximity: 2**

### Darling, R., Hansen, K., Aguilera, R., Basu, R., Benmarhnia, T., & Letellier, N. (2023). The burden of wildfire smoke on respiratory health in California at the zip code level: Uncovering the disproportionate impacts of differential fine particle composition. *GeoHealth*, 7(10), e2023GH000884.
Using zip-code-level data for California (2006--2019), the authors find that ignoring differential toxicity of wildfire PM2.5 underestimates the respiratory health burden by approximately 13%, with greater underestimation in high social vulnerability index (SVI) areas. This study bridges the epidemiology and environmental justice categories and provides direct evidence that BenMAP-CE's assumption of equal PM2.5 toxicity across sources leads to underestimation in vulnerable communities.
**Proximity: 4**

## Category 4: Economic Valuation of Air Pollution Health Effects

### Deryugina, T., Heutel, G., Miller, N.H., Molitor, D., & Reif, J. (2019). The mortality and medical costs of air pollution: Evidence from changes in wind direction. *American Economic Review*, 109(12), 4178--4219.
Using wind direction as an instrument for PM2.5 exposure among elderly Medicare beneficiaries, the authors estimate causal effects of acute particulate matter exposure on mortality, healthcare use, and medical costs. The study finds mortality effects concentrated in about 25% of the elderly population. Published in the AER, this provides the gold-standard causal identification of air pollution health costs and establishes the general framework for monetizing pollution-related health impacts.
**Proximity: 3**

### Fann, N., Lamson, A.D., Anenberg, S.C., Wesson, K., Risley, D., & Hubbell, B.J. (2012). Estimating the national public health burden associated with exposure to ambient PM2.5 and ozone. *Risk Analysis*, 32(1), 81--95.
This EPA study estimates 130,000 PM2.5-related deaths and 4,700 ozone-related deaths from 2005 air quality levels using CMAQ modeling combined with monitored data. The study demonstrates the BenMAP-CE approach to quantifying health burden at scale and provides the national context for understanding the magnitude of air pollution health costs relative to the wildfire-specific subset.
**Proximity: 3**

### Connolly, R., Marlier, M.E., Garcia-Gonzales, D.A., Wilkins, J., Su, J., Bekker, C., Jung, J., Bonilla, E., Burnett, R.T., Zhu, Y., & Jerrett, M. (2024). Mortality attributable to PM2.5 from wildland fires in California from 2008 to 2018. *Science Advances*, 10(24), eadl1252.
The study estimates 52,480--55,710 premature deaths attributable to wildland fire PM2.5 over 11 years, equating to $432--456 billion in economic impact. Notably, the study applies a wildfire-specific chronic dose-response coefficient accounting for differential toxicity. While focused on mortality rather than ED visits, this California-specific wildfire valuation study provides important context for the relative magnitude of morbidity costs and demonstrates the feasibility of wildfire-specific valuation in California.
**Proximity: 4**

### Rappold, A.G., Reyes, J., Pouliot, G., Cascio, W.E., & Diaz-Sanchez, D. (2017). Community vulnerability to health impacts of wildland fire smoke exposure. *Environmental Science & Technology*, 51(12), 6674--6682.
The authors develop a Community Health-Vulnerability Index (CHVI) incorporating asthma prevalence, COPD, hypertension, diabetes, obesity, elderly population share, and socioeconomic indicators. Five principal components explain 84% of variance. The CHVI framework is relevant for understanding how the economic burden of wildfire smoke ED visits may be distributed unevenly across communities, informing the environmental justice dimension of the capstone.
**Proximity: 3**

### Miller, N.H., Molitor, D., & Zou, E. (2024). The nonlinear effects of air pollution on health: Evidence from wildfire smoke. NBER Working Paper No. 32924.
Using administrative Medicare records for 2007--2019, the authors estimate that wildfire smoke accounts for 18% of ambient PM2.5 concentrations, 0.42% of deaths, and 0.69% of emergency room visits among adults aged 65 and over. Critically, they find concave concentration-response relationships: smaller pollution shocks have outsized health impacts, suggesting significant health benefits from improving air quality even in areas meeting current regulatory standards. This finding has direct implications for how BenMAP-CE's linear dose-response functions may mischaracterize the health burden of wildfire smoke, complementing the Heft-Neal et al. (2023) nonlinearity findings with Medicare administrative data.
**Proximity: 4**

### Qiu, M., Callahan, C.W., Higuera-Mendieta, I., Rennels, L., Parthum, B., Diffenbaugh, N.S., & Burke, M. (2026). Valuing wildfire smoke-related mortality benefits from climate mitigation. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 123(8), e2533772123.
The authors develop an integrated framework combining econometric smoke-mortality relationships, machine learning climate-smoke projections, and negative feedback estimates between current and future wildfire activity. They project that 3 degrees C of future warming will lead to 46,200 annual smoke-related deaths in the U.S., doubling current levels. They estimate a partial social cost of carbon of $15.1 (95% CI: $2.5--$49.3) per tonne CO2 from wildfire smoke mortality alone, which would double current U.S. domestic social cost of carbon estimates. While focused on mortality rather than morbidity, this paper demonstrates state-of-the-art wildfire smoke valuation methods and underscores the growing economic significance of smoke health impacts.
**Proximity: 3**

## Category 5: Environmental Justice

### Masri, S., Scaduto, E., Jin, Y., & Wu, J. (2021). Disproportionate impacts of wildfires among elderly and low-income communities in California from 2000--2020. *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*, 18(8), 3921.
Examining California census tracts from 2000--2020, the study finds that areas with higher fire frequency and burned area had higher proportions of elderly and low-income residents, lower median household incomes, and greater proportions of Native American populations. These findings demonstrate that wildfire impacts in California are not equally distributed, providing the equity rationale for accurate economic valuation of smoke-attributable ED visits across demographic groups.
**Proximity: 3**

### Liu, J., Clark, L.P., Bechle, M.J., Hajat, A., Kim, S.-Y., Robinson, A.L., Sheppard, L., Szpiro, A.A., & Marshall, J.D. (2021). Disparities in air pollution exposure in the United States by race/ethnicity and income, 1990--2010. *Environmental Health Perspectives*, 129(12), 127005.
This study finds that for all years and all six criteria pollutants studied, the racial/ethnic group with the highest national average exposure was always a minority group. For PM2.5 in 2010, non-Hispanic Black populations had exposures at least 5% above average in 63% of states. Absolute exposure disparities were 1.1--21x larger across racial/ethnic groups than across income categories. This broader air pollution equity evidence contextualizes wildfire-specific disparities.
**Proximity: 2**

### Tessum, C.W., Paolella, D.A., Chambliss, S.E., Apte, J.S., Hill, J.D., & Marshall, J.D. (2021). PM2.5 polluters disproportionately and systemically affect people of color in the United States. *Science Advances*, 7(18), eabf4491.
The authors show that nearly all major emission categories disproportionately expose people of color to PM2.5, consistently across states, urban/rural areas, income levels, and exposure levels. This systemic pattern applies to wildfire smoke as well and strengthens the environmental justice argument for ensuring that economic valuations of wildfire smoke health impacts adequately capture costs borne by minority communities.
**Proximity: 2**

### Tessum, C.W., Apte, J.S., Goodkind, A.L., Muller, N.Z., Mullins, K.A., Paolella, D.A., Polasky, S., Springer, N.P., Thakrar, S.K., Marshall, J.D., & Hill, J.D. (2019). Inequity in consumption of goods and services adds to racial-ethnic disparities in air pollution exposure. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 116(13), 6001--6006.
This study finds that non-Hispanic whites experience approximately 17% less PM2.5 exposure than caused by their consumption, while Black and Hispanic populations bear approximately 56% and 63% excess exposure respectively. This consumption-pollution-exposure framework provides a structural explanation for environmental inequity that extends to wildfire smoke contexts and informs why accurate economic valuation matters for policy equity.
**Proximity: 2**

### Han, X., Li, W., & Wang, H. (2024). A burning issue: Wildfire smoke exposure, retail sales, and demand for adaptation in healthcare. *Environmental and Resource Economics*, 87(11), 3011--3039.
Using NielsenIQ retail scanner data (2006--2019), the authors find that wildfire smoke exposure significantly increases retail sales of air purifiers, cold remedies, nasal products, and other health-related products, with effects varying by social vulnerability. This study documents the defensive spending dimension of wildfire smoke costs -- expenditures that are captured by WTP but not by COI measures in BenMAP-CE -- and reveals that adaptation capacity varies with socioeconomic status.
**Proximity: 4**
